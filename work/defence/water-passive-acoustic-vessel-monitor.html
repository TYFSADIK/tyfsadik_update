Water: Passive Acoustic Vessel Monitor

A dock-mounted hydrophone node that listens to the water, detects and classifies passing vessels from their machinery and propeller signatures, and uses an RTL-SDR AIS receiver as ground truth - solar powered, receive-only, and built to buffer through dead links.

2026 Defence Hardware In Progress

Overview

This is the water node of my defence hardware program: a hydrophone hanging off a dock (or, later, a mooring), a Raspberry Pi 5 that records and classifies what it hears, and a cheap AIS receiver that tells me what actually went past so the classifier can be trained and scored against reality. Every vessel passage becomes a labelled acoustic event: spectrogram in, vessel class and direction out.

The Canadian context is direct. The Canadian Arctic Sensor Suite Initiative aims for persistent surveillance from seabed to space, and the underwater leg of that is the hard one. DRDC's Northern Watch demonstration ran into problems with installation, ice, technical issues, and data management - the unglamorous parts, not the sensing itself. And in DRDC-linked work at Dalhousie, uncrewed vehicles act as sensor hosts that dock at seabed nodes to recharge and download data, while other DRDC-related work pairs hydrophone arrays with AIS data and ferry GPS as ground truth. This build copies exactly those two patterns at hobby scale: AIS as the labelling source, and a dock-and-upload extension as the roadmap.

The node emits nothing. It listens to the water and listens to the 162 MHz AIS band - both receive-only - which keeps it legal, low-power, and hard to detect. What it practices is the layer Northern Watch struggled with: cheap nodes that stay alive, manage their own data, and sync when a link finally appears.

How I Built It

01
Capture chain: hydrophone to Pi

The hydrophone feeds a USB audio interface, which the Pi 5 sees as a standard ALSA device. For the XLR version of the H2d I enable the interface's 48 V phantom power; the 3.5 mm A5 variant needs no phantom. Recording is plain Linux tooling: arecord for scripted captures (arecord -D hw:1,0 -f S32_LE -r 96000) and sox/rec for quick clips, at 48-96 kHz depending on how much high-frequency content I want for propeller harmonics.

The higher sample rate matters: small-boat propellers and outboards put useful energy well above 20 kHz, and the 96 kHz capture keeps that in band for the classifier.

02
Bench validation in the test tank

Before any field deployment, the whole chain gets validated in the glass test tank on my bench - the setup in the photo above. I play recorded vessel signatures and swept tones through a speaker coupled to the tank, check the noise floor, verify the phantom-powered path against the 3.5 mm path, and confirm the spectrogram pipeline sees what it should. If it cannot hear a known signal in a tank, it has no business on a dock.

03
AIS ground truth

The RTL-SDR Blog V4 with a 162 MHz marine antenna runs rtl_ais (with AISdecoder as the alternative demodulator) covering both AIS channels at 161.975 and 162.025 MHz. Every decoded message gets logged with MMSI, position, speed, course, and timestamp.

That log is the labelling machine. A strong acoustic event whose window lines up with an AIS track passing the node becomes a labelled sample - vessel type from the AIS static data, approach versus departure from the range trend. This mirrors the DRDC-related work that pairs hydrophone arrays with AIS and ferry GPS as ground truth, minus the budget.

04
Features and classification

Audio windows become mel spectrograms with librosa (and scipy.signal for narrowband line tracking of propeller blade-rate harmonics). A small classifier - deliberately small, it has to run on the Pi 5 - predicts vessel class from the labelled dataset built in step 03.

Direction comes free from physics: an approaching vessel's signature shows a slight Doppler upshift that relaxes downward at closest point of approach, and the amplitude envelope rises then falls. The classifier scores approach/departure from those two cues and the AIS log grades the answer.

05
Field housing and power

Field kit is an IP67 box with cable glands for the hydrophone lead, antenna feed, and solar input. The hydrophone hangs on a weighted assembly so it sits below the surface and away from dock slap. Power is a 12 V battery charged by a 30-50 W panel through an MPPT controller, with a DC-DC buck delivering clean 5 V to the Pi 5 - sized for grey Canadian weeks, not best-case sun.

06
Store-and-forward sync

Detections, classifier confidence, and short audio snippets buffer locally on high-endurance storage (microSD now, NVMe on the M.2 HAT as the dataset grows). When a link is up, the node publishes signed detection messages over MQTT to the multi-domain common operating picture; when the link is down, it keeps collecting and syncs the backlog on reconnect.

That store-and-forward discipline is the same pattern I work through in DDIL-Proof DevOps - the water node is one of its field test cases.

07
Extension: the docking node

The roadmap copies the Dalhousie pattern: mount the sensor package on a small autonomous boat that patrols, then returns to a dock node to recharge and upload its data physically - the seabed docking-node concept, at pond-and-lake scale. The dock node reuses everything above: same buffer format, same MQTT sync, just a hull that comes to the data link instead of waiting for one.

Testing & Metrics

The test protocol has three phases, each with defined targets; results will be published with the dataset as runs complete.

  • Tank validation (bench): known signals played into the test tank - swept tones and recorded vessel signatures. Target: the capture chain reproduces the reference spectrogram with no dropouts at 48 kHz and 96 kHz, and the noise floor stays stable across an overnight soak.
  • Field detection vs AIS (dock deployment): over multi-day collection windows, every AIS-logged vessel passage within the node's listening area is checked against the acoustic event log. Metrics: detection rate (fraction of AIS passages with a matching acoustic event), false-event rate per hour, and timestamp offset between acoustic detection and AIS position.
  • Classification scoring: on the labelled dataset, per-class precision/recall for vessel type and approach/departure direction, reported as a confusion matrix - no single headline number.

As of writing, tank validation is running and field collection has not started; no detection-rate numbers exist yet and I won't quote any until the AIS-scored windows are done.

Passive listening only: this node never transmits in the marine bands - not on VHF, not acoustically. It receives AIS and listens to the water, and that is all. Transmitting to interfere with marine radio or navigation systems is illegal in Canada and has no place here.
Privacy: the classifier is tuned to machinery and propeller signatures - engine harmonics, blade-rate lines, broadband cavitation. Voices are not a target and are not analyzed; the features the pipeline extracts (spectrograms, narrowband lines, envelopes) are poor at speech by design.

Status & Roadmap

  • In progress: tank validation soak tests; labelling pipeline that joins acoustic events to AIS tracks; first classifier training on the accumulating labelled dataset; IP67 housing and weighted hydrophone assembly.

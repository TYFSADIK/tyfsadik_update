Convoy-Following Robots on a Degraded Mesh

Two rovers convoy behind a lead vehicle over a 915 MHz LoRa / Wi-Fi mesh, hold formation through induced link drops, and recover on reconnect - the Army resupply-convoy and DDIL communications problem, reproduced on my bench.

2026 Defence Hardware In Progress

Overview

This build is a two-rover convoy that keeps behaving when the radio link gets bad. A lead rover drives a course while broadcasting its position, heading, and speed over a 915 MHz LoRa link (with Wi-Fi as a fallback path); the follower runs a gap-following controller that holds a set distance and offset behind it. Then I degrade the link on purpose and measure what the convoy does: how formation error grows with packet loss, how the follower behaves when the link drops entirely, and how fast it rejoins when the link comes back.

Why this specific problem: the Defence Drone Initiative (launched July 23, 2026) names ground robots for resupply, casualty evacuation, and terrain too hazardous for personnel as a first-priority area, and DRDC tests convoying and resilient operation on exercises like Operation NANOOK. A resupply convoy that falls apart the moment the radio link degrades is useless - in the North, on a damaged road, or under deliberate interference, the link will degrade. The interesting engineering is not the following; it is the denied, degraded, intermittent, and limited (DDIL) behaviour: what the machine does in the gap between "link fine" and "link dead," and how gracefully it recovers.

Everything here is already bought and on my bench. The chassis, compute, and drive electronics are shared with my GPS-denied navigation rover, and the LoRa boards come from the same Phase 3 mesh stock as the seismic perimeter nodes - so this build reuses proven hardware and adds the convoy link, the follow controller, and a disciplined degraded-link test protocol.

Figure 1: the lead broadcasts its pose at a fixed rate over LoRa (with an impaired Wi-Fi fallback); the follower's watchdog arbitrates the link, and the controller drives through the e-stop interlock at all times.

Both paths carry the same packet format, so the follower does not care which radio delivered the update - the watchdog simply tracks the age of the newest good packet from either path. That is the DDIL lesson in miniature: degrade gracefully across heterogeneous links instead of betting the convoy on one of them.

How I Built It

01
Second rover: clone the proven drive stack

Rover R2 is a deliberate copy of R1: the same metal 4WD chassis with wheel encoders, a Pi 5 8 GB with an active cooler running Ubuntu 24.04, and a BTS7960 dual H-bridge driver. The e-stop is a physical latching switch wired so it cuts motor power at the driver, not in software - software can lie, a cut enable line cannot.

The drive stack is ROS 2 (Jazzy) with the same differential-drive teleop and odometry nodes I already tuned on the GPS-denied rover: encoder ticks feed wheel odometry, and a BNO085-class IMU feeds the heading estimate. Cloning first means any convoy weirdness I see later is a convoy problem, not a "why is this rover different" problem.

02
Convoy link: ESP32 LoRa boards at 915 MHz

Each rover carries an ESP32 LoRa board (Heltec V3 / LilyGO T-Beam class, from the Phase 3 mesh stock) flashed with Arduino-IDE firmware built on the RadioLib SX1262 driver. The lead broadcasts a compact binary packet - rover ID, timestamp, x/y pose, heading, speed, and a CRC - at a fixed rate; the follower's board receives over UART to its Pi.

The link deliberately runs at conservative LoRa settings (higher spreading factor, modest bandwidth) because range and robustness matter here, not throughput - a convoy update is a few dozen bytes. The Wi-Fi path carries the identical packet over UDP so the two links can be compared and impaired independently.

03
Follow controller: gap-following, pure-pursuit style

The follower computes a goal point at a set following distance and lateral offset behind the lead's reported pose, then tracks it with a pure-pursuit-style controller: the curvature to the goal point is computed from the follower's own odometry/IMU estimate, and linear speed is scaled down as the gap error closes so the follower never rear-ends the lead on a hard brake.

Because the lead's pose arrives over a lossy link, the controller consumes the newest packet with its age, not a presumed-perfect stream: packets older than a configured freshness threshold are extrapolated briefly and then distrusted. That single design decision is most of what makes the degraded-link behaviour sane.

04
Degrade the link on purpose

On the Wi-Fi fallback path I add controlled impairment with Linux traffic control: tc qdisc add dev wlan0 root netem loss 20% delay 150ms and variations - random loss, added latency, jitter, and rate throttling - so I can dial in an exact degradation profile and repeat it run after run.

On the LoRa path there is no netem, so I degrade at the source: the lead firmware duty-cycles its transmissions (drop every nth packet, or hold for a configurable blackout window). Every run logs packet loss on both links alongside the controller's gap error, so "link quality vs control quality" is a measured curve, not a vibe.

05
Loss behaviour: hold, stop, rejoin

The link watchdog on the follower implements a three-state policy. Link healthy: follow normally. Link timeout: hold the last-known formation vector briefly - keep tracking the extrapolated goal for a short, fixed grace window while decelerating. Grace expired: a controlled safe-stop, not an emergency lock-up, and hold position.

On reconnect the follower does not snap to the lead's current position; it re-acquires the track, plans a rejoin from where it actually is, and closes the gap at limited speed. Recovery time after link loss - from first good packet back to within formation tolerance - is a headline metric, because that is exactly what a resupply convoy commander would ask about.

06
Payload run: convoy laps with induced outages

With formation holding reliably, the follower carries the dummy payload - a weighted box standing in for resupply cargo - strapped over the chassis centreline so the extra mass changes the dynamics the way a real load would: longer braking distance, more lag in the gap controller.

Test runs are convoy laps in a clear area with scripted link outages injected mid-lap (netem on Wi-Fi, blackout windows on LoRa), so every lap contains at least one full hold-stop-rejoin cycle under load.

07
Metrics: following error, recovery time, loss tolerance

Each rover logs odometry, link stats, and controller state to rosbag plus plain CSV, and the laptop topology view (visible in the bench photo) shows live per-link status. Post-run, a small Python analysis script (pandas + matplotlib) turns the logs into the three headline numbers below.

The metrics I publish are: following error (lateral and along-track gap error vs induced packet-loss rate), recovery time after a full link loss, and packet-loss tolerance - the sustained loss rate at which the watchdog forces a safe-stop.

Testing & Metrics

The test protocol is built around repeatable, scripted impairment rather than "drive around and see what happens":

  • Baseline laps: clean link on both radios, payload off then on; establishes the formation-error floor the degraded runs are compared against.
  • Loss sweep: netem loss stepped in stages (light to severe) on the Wi-Fi path and duty-cycled dropouts on LoRa; following error is logged at each stage to build the loss-vs-error curve.
  • Blackout runs: full link loss of fixed durations mid-lap; measures grace-window behaviour, safe-stop distance, and recovery time on reconnect.
  • Payload repeats: the loss sweep and blackout runs repeated with the weighted payload, quantifying how cargo mass changes braking distance and rejoin behaviour.

Targets, not results: the design goal is stable formation through moderate sustained loss, a safe-stop within a short distance of the hold point on full loss, and a rejoin that needs no human input. Measured numbers from the loss sweep and blackout runs are in progress and will be published here with the CSV logs and plots once the payload runs are complete - I am not publishing figures before the data exists.

Safety & Canada Notes

Physical safety: both rovers carry physical e-stop switches that cut motor power at the driver, and every convoy run happens in a clear area with nobody inside the course. A follower that misbehaves on a bad link must be stoppable by hand, instantly - that is the point of the exercise, not an inconvenience.
Radio rules: the LoRa hardware is 915 MHz, the correct North American / Canadian ISM band - 868 MHz parts are for Europe. Power and duty cycle stay within licence-exempt limits.
Degraded-link testing ethics: all link impairment is done on my own radios only - tc netem on my own Wi-Fi path, and duty-cycled transmissions from my own LoRa firmware. Nothing here jams, interferes with, or even touches anyone else's signals; jamming is illegal in Canada and would also invalidate the data.

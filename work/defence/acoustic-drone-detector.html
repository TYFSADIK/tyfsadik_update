Passive Acoustic Drone Detector

A MEMS microphone array on a Raspberry Pi that listens for the harmonic comb of drone rotors, rejects birds and aircraft, and estimates direction of arrival by beamforming - emitting nothing at all.

2026 Defence Hardware In Progress

Overview

NRC and DRDC have built a counter-drone detection system that picks up the signature of a drone's rotating propellers without emitting any radio signal that could reveal its own location. This build is that idea at hobby scale: a ReSpeaker-class 4-mic USB array on a Raspberry Pi 5 that does nothing but listen. It emits nothing, so it cannot be detected, jammed into revealing itself, or geolocated by an adversary's RF sensors.

Small multirotor drones announce themselves acoustically whether they want to or not. Each rotor spins at a fixed rate per flight phase, producing a fundamental blade-pass frequency in the ~100-500 Hz band plus a comb of evenly spaced harmonics that persists as long as the motors run. Birds flap (intermittent, broadband), fixed-wing aircraft drone on much lower frequencies at much greater distance, and traffic noise is broadband and unstructured. That harmonic comb is a fingerprint, and it is what this sensor keys on.

The context is the same one driving the whole program: Canada's Defence Drone Initiative (launched July 23, 2026) and the Army MINERVA challenges both assume small drones are cheap, common, and hard to see. A receive-only acoustic node is the cheapest possible layer of counter-drone awareness - it costs about as much as a dinner out, runs off a USB power bank, and feeds its detections into the same common operating picture as my passive radar node.

The real rig on my bench: the circular 4-mic MEMS array on a stand, wired to the Raspberry Pi running the DSP chain. The two analysis branches share one spectrogram stage. Detection and classification work on the summed audio (best signal-to-noise), while direction of arrival works on the phase differences between channels. Raw audio is processed in memory in short windows and discarded - only detection events with azimuth, timestamp, and confidence are kept.

How I Built It

01
Array bring-up

The ReSpeaker-class 4-mic USB array enumerates on the Pi 5 as a standard USB Audio Class device - no vendor drivers needed. I confirmed it with arecord -l, then captured all four channels at once with arecord -D hw:1,0 -f S32_LE -r 48000 -c 4 test.wav and inspected the WAV channel by channel in Python (scipy.io.wavfile) to make sure no channel is dead or swapped.

Channel sync is the thing that makes beamforming possible: all four mics must sample from the same clock, which they do on this class of array (one shared ADC stream). I verified it empirically - play a tone from a speaker at a known position and confirm the inter-channel delays are small, stable, and consistent with the mic geometry.

02
Signal chain: spectrograms and the harmonic comb

The DSP pipeline runs in Python with NumPy/SciPy: audio arrives in ~1-second windows, each window gets a Hann-windowed STFT (scipy.signal.stft, 4096-point FFT for ~12 Hz bins at 48 kHz), and the magnitude spectrogram is converted to dB. A noise floor is tracked per bin with a slow moving average so detection adapts to wind and distant traffic.

Rotor detection looks for the comb: a candidate fundamental in the ~100-500 Hz band, then a check for evenly spaced peaks at 2x, 3x, 4x... that persist across consecutive windows. A single-frame peak is not enough - rotors hold their speed for seconds, which is exactly what separates them from birdsong chirps.

03
Classification: drone vs bird vs aircraft

Comb detections become feature vectors: fundamental frequency, number of visible harmonics, comb persistence time, harmonic-to-noise ratio, and bandwidth. Birds fail on persistence and spacing; distant fixed-wing aircraft sit below the drone band and fail on fundamental range; helicopters are a genuinely hard case and get their own class label for now.

I started with hand-set thresholds, then a small SVM (scikit-learn) trained on recordings of my own drones plus negative samples (park ambience, traffic, birds, a lawnmower - the classic false friend). The escape hatch is a compact 1D CNN or tiny spectrogram CNN exported to ONNX if the SVM's false-alarm rate won't come down; the Pi 5 has the headroom for either.

04
Direction of arrival by beamforming

With four mics in a known circular geometry, azimuth falls out of the phase differences between channels. I use GCC-PHAT (generalized cross-correlation with phase transform) to estimate time delays between mic pairs, then SRP-PHAT (steered response power) to scan candidate azimuths and pick the direction that maximizes steered energy. PHAT weighting whitens the spectrum, which is what makes it robust against the comb's narrowband peaks.

DoA is computed only on windows where the classifier says "drone", which keeps the polar plot clean. The tracker draws azimuth against time on a polar display (matplotlib on the dev laptop; a lightweight web dashboard for the field node), with the comb fundamental shown alongside - that is the view in the bench photo, locked near 660 Hz on one of my quads.

05
Test protocol

Test targets are my own drones only, plus recorded samples - never other people's aircraft. The protocol: hover and fly-past runs at measured distances with a line of sight, logging detection yes/no per run, first-detection distance, and DoA error against the drone's true bearing. Negative-source runs (park, traffic, birds, lawn equipment) log false-alarm rate per hour.

Every run records conditions (wind, background noise, drone model, throttle state) so results are comparable over time. Results stay unpublished until there are enough runs to be honest - the numbers below are stated as protocol and targets, not measurements.

06
Reporting into the common operating picture

Detections are published over MQTT as signed JSON events (node ID, timestamp, azimuth, comb fundamental, confidence, signature) using the same signing discipline as the rest of the fleet. The broker feeds the multi-domain common operating picture, where this node's acoustic tracks are fused with the passive radar node's RF tracks - two sensors that share the property of emitting nothing.

The node buffers detections locally when the link drops and forwards on reconnect, following the same store-and-forward pattern as the other field nodes. The simulation sibling of this fusion work is the counter-drone sensor fusion simulator.

Testing & Metrics

The test protocol and what is being measured:

  • Detection rate vs distance: fraction of runs with a detection, bucketed by slant range. Target: a clear, repeatable detection envelope against my own quads in quiet suburban conditions; the specific distances stay unpublished until the run count is honest.
  • DoA accuracy: angular error between the estimated azimuth and the drone's true bearing (from its logged GPS track). Target: useful sector-level accuracy - tens of degrees is enough to cue a camera or a second sensor.
  • False-alarm rate: alarms per hour against negative sources (birds, traffic, lawn equipment, voices). Target: low enough that a human reviewing the alert queue is not numbed by noise; measured, not assumed.
  • Classification confusion: the bird / drone / aircraft confusion matrix on held-out recordings, tracked per classifier version.

All measured results are in progress: the bench rig detects and tracks rotor harmonics (see the photo above), but field-range numbers will be published only after a proper run series, not from a handful of anecdotes.

Legal & Safety Notes

Privacy by design: the pipeline keys on narrow-band rotor harmonics and discards everything else. It is not a microphone for speech and records no conversations - audio windows are processed in memory and thrown away; only detection events (azimuth, frequency, timestamp, confidence) are stored.
Detection only: this node senses and reports. No jamming, no spoofing - both are illegal in Canada. The whole point of the receive-only design is that it never transmits anything at all.

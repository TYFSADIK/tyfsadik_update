Sky: Passive Aerial Early-Warning Node

A receive-only passive radar node built on a KrakenSDR five-channel coherent SDR: it listens to reflections of existing FM and UHF TV broadcasts bouncing off aircraft, computes range-Doppler plots with pyAPRiL, and scores every detection against an RTL-SDR ADS-B ground-truth receiver. It emits nothing.

2026 Defence Hardware In Progress

Overview

Canada's Arctic Over-the-Horizon Radar (A-OTHR) - based on Australia's JORN design, adapted for RCAF/NORAD, with initial capability due December 2029 - will provide long-range early-warning coverage of the northern approaches as one layer in a sensor picture that also includes conventional radar, airborne, and space-based sensors. Its companion program, Crossbow, is a classified Canada-US non-radar sensor network. You can't copy Crossbow, and you shouldn't try.

What you can build is the open, hobby-scale, receive-only analogue: a passive radar node. Instead of transmitting its own pulses, passive radar listens to reflections of existing broadcast signals - FM radio (88-108 MHz) and UHF TV (~470-600 MHz) - bouncing off aircraft. Because it emits nothing, it is legally clean, RF-silent (it reveals no location of its own), and cheap. A KrakenSDR gives me five phase-coherent receive channels, which is enough to point one antenna at the broadcast transmitter (the reference channel) and the rest at the sky (the surveillance channels), then cross-correlate the two to find echoes.

This is Phase 1 of my Canadian Defence Hardware Program. It is the flagship sky-domain node: every detection it makes will eventually publish over MQTT into the multi-domain common operating picture, fused with the water and ground nodes.

How I Built It

01
ADS-B ground truth first (~US$100)

Before any radar work, I put up the truth receiver: an RTL-SDR Blog V4 with a 1090 MHz antenna, running the readsb/dump1090-fa decoder on the mini PC, with tar1090 serving a live aircraft map in the browser. The decoder writes aircraft positions (lat, lon, altitude, heading, speed, ICAO24) to JSON history files, and I log those continuously.

This log is the scoring key for everything that follows: for any timestamp, I can say exactly which cooperative aircraft were actually in the airspace and where.

02
Illuminator survey

Passive radar is only as good as its illuminators of opportunity, so I mapped my local broadcast landscape: FM stations in 88-108 MHz and UHF TV transmitters around 470-600 MHz, noting each one's frequency, bearing, and received power with the RTL-SDR and a simple spectrum scan. FM gives wide bandwidth-modulated content and long range; UHF TV (ATSC) gives better range resolution.

I tuned the two directional antennas - a Yagi for the FM band and a log-periodic covering UHF - with the NanoVNA, checking SWR across each target band and trimming element lengths where needed before committing to a mount.

03
KrakenSDR bring-up

The KrakenSDR is five RTL-SDR-class coherent receivers in one box, covering 24-1766 MHz with a shared clock so all channels stay phase-aligned - the property that makes direction-finding and multi-channel passive radar possible at this price. I run the open-source KrakenSDR software stack on the mini PC (a Raspberry Pi 4/5 also works) with the krakensdr_doa software and its GNU Radio-companion DSP chain.

The critical step is phase calibration: the unit has a built-in noise source that injects a common signal into all five channels, and the software measures and nulls out per-channel phase and amplitude offsets. I re-run this calibration after any frequency change or cable swap, because correlation quality collapses if the channels drift out of alignment.

04
Passive radar chain

Two channels carry the geometry. The reference channel points a Yagi directly at the broadcast transmitter and captures the clean direct signal. The surveillance channel points at the area of interest and captures that same signal delayed and Doppler-shifted by reflections off aircraft. Cross-correlating surveillance against reference over a bank of Doppler shifts produces a range-Doppler map: bistatic range on one axis, Doppler speed on the other, with aircraft appearing as peaks above the clutter ridge at zero Doppler.

The bistatic geometry matters: range here is the extra path length (transmitter-to-aircraft-to-receiver minus the direct path), so converting a detection into a map position requires knowing the transmitter location and solving the bistatic ellipse - which is exactly what the processing library in the next step handles.

05
Processing with pyAPRiL

For the heavy DSP I use pyAPRiL, an open-source Python passive-radar library available on PyPI. It implements the full chain: coherent processing interval selection, the cross-ambiguity function for range-Doppler surfaces, clutter filtering (suppressing the direct-path breakthrough and stationary echoes that otherwise swamp aircraft returns), direction-of-arrival estimation across the coherent channels, and target localization from the bistatic geometry.

My pipeline streams IQ from the KrakenSDR into pyAPRiL processing blocks on the mini PC's Ubuntu install, with NumPy under the hood; the N100-class CPU keeps up at moderate sample rates, and the 16 GB of RAM matters more than cores because range-Doppler surfaces accumulate in memory.

06
Scoring against ADS-B truth

Every candidate detection from the range-Doppler processing gets compared against the logged ADS-B truth from step 01. For each ADS-B aircraft in view, I compute where it should appear in bistatic range-Doppler space given the illuminator geometry, then check whether the radar actually produced a detection there - and whether it produced detections where no aircraft was.

That yields the three numbers that define a radar: detection rate, false-alarm rate, and effective range per illuminator. FM versus UHF TV get scored separately, because the honest answer is usually "it depends on the transmitter."

07
Multi-node fusion

One node gives range-Doppler blips; two or three nodes at different locations give positions by intersection. The plan is to stand up additional nodes and have each publish its detections over MQTT - the same signed-detections bus the rest of the hardware program uses - into the multi-domain common operating picture, where a Kalman-filter tracker fuses them with the water and ground node outputs.

Store-and-forward applies here too: nodes buffer detections through dead links and sync on reconnect, the DDIL pattern the rest of the program is built around.

Testing & Metrics

The test protocol is built around the ADS-B ground truth, and every run is logged so results are reproducible rather than anecdotal:

  • Detection rate: for each logged ADS-B aircraft track, did the passive radar produce a corresponding range-Doppler detection within tolerance? Reported as a percentage per illuminator (FM vs UHF TV) and per range band.
  • False-alarm rate: detections with no matching ADS-B track, per hour of observation. Clutter-filter tuning is judged mostly on this number.
  • Effective range per illuminator: the furthest bistatic range at which the detection rate stays useful, measured against ADS-B positions - reported per transmitter, not as one number.
  • Calibration stability: how long phase calibration holds before range-Doppler quality measurably degrades, which sets the re-calibration interval.

Results are in progress and not yet published. The ADS-B truth log has been accumulating since the receiver went live; the range-Doppler chain is producing plots on the bench, and the scored detection/false-alarm dataset will be published once I have enough scored hours per illuminator to make the numbers meaningful. I am deliberately not quoting range or detection-rate figures until then.

Legal & Safety Notes

Export control: the KrakenSDR team once pulled its passive-radar code because it appeared to fall under an export-control category covering passive radar. Before publishing radar code, I check Canada's export control rules with Global Affairs Canada - or publish only the write-up, the data, and the metrics. Handling that carefully is itself a good signal to security reviewers.
Receive-only: everything on this node receives; it never transmits. Jamming is illegal in Canada, and nothing here is capable of interfering with any signal - it only listens to broadcasts that already exist.

Canadian Defence Hardware Program

Eleven hands-on hardware builds across sky, water, and ground, each one mapped to a stated Canadian Armed Forces requirement - with full bills of materials, prices, step-by-step build guides, and bench photos of the real rigs.

Why This Program Exists

In 2026 Canada started saying, in public documents, exactly what it wants built. I read those documents as free requirements specs and started building against them in my own lab:

  • Defence Drone Initiative (DDI) - launched July 23, 2026. First priority areas: low-cost tactical ISR drones, and ground robots for resupply, casualty evacuation, and terrain too hazardous for personnel.
  • Army MINERVA challenges - the Army states it has bought foreign robotic systems of mixed quality, with problems in cold-weather operation and components from unstable supply chains, and that it currently lacks a drone that can do ISR in Arctic conditions. The Arctic drone call closed September 24, 2026; the full text reads like an evaluator's checklist.
  • DRDC field trials - GPS-denied navigation, convoying, cold-weather performance, and resilient operation, exercised on events like Operation NANOOK.
  • NRC + DRDC counter-drone work - a detection system that picks up the signature of a drone's rotating propellers without emitting any radio signal that could reveal its own location.
  • Airspace deconfliction - the Army is studying how to manage airspace shared by many uncrewed aircraft at once.

These calls are aimed at companies, not students - so I treat them as requirements documents, not application forms. Every build below answers one of them at hobby scale, with real hardware, real measurements, and a published dataset or dashboard at the end.

Requirement Source 1: Defence Drone Initiative (DDI)

The DDI, launched July 23, 2026, names its first priority areas plainly: low-cost tactical ISR drones, and ground robots for resupply, casualty evacuation, and terrain too hazardous for personnel. Read as a spec, that is three requirements: cheap enough to be attritable, useful sensors on board, and autonomy that keeps working when the operator link does not. The thermal SAR drone answers the ISR line - an X500-class airframe with edge detection so the platform is useful even with a degraded downlink. The GPS-denied rover and convoy robots answer the ground-robot line, with the emphasis on continuing the mission through link loss rather than stopping and waiting. The DDI also talks about supply-chain integrity for uncrewed systems, which is the requirement the secure fleet updates build takes on directly: secure boot, TPM, signed OTA images, and automatic rollback.

Requirement Source 2: Army MINERVA Challenges

The MINERVA challenge text is unusually candid: the Army says it has bought foreign robotic systems of mixed quality, with problems in cold-weather operation and components from unstable supply chains, and that it currently lacks a drone able to do ISR in Arctic conditions. The Arctic drone call closed September 24, 2026, and the full text reads like an evaluator's checklist - endurance in the cold, sensors that work in snow and low light, operation alongside Canadian Rangers. Two of my builds map straight onto it: the cold-soak characterization rig attacks the stated cold-weather pain point head-on by publishing runtime, voltage sag, and failure-mode data against temperature, and the thermal SAR drone matches the Arctic-sovereignty and SAR-with-Rangers requirement with a thermal camera and on-board person detection. The supply-chain complaint is covered by the secure-updates build above.

Requirement Source 3: DRDC Field Trials and Operation NANOOK

DRDC's field-trial pattern - exercised on events like Operation NANOOK - keeps returning to the same four themes: GPS-denied navigation, convoying, cold-weather performance, and resilient operation when communications are degraded. These are trial scenarios, so my builds mirror them as test protocols rather than as products: the GPS-denied rover cuts GNSS deliberately and measures EKF drift against recorded ground truth; the convoy robots hold formation through induced link drops on a LoRa/Wi-Fi mesh; and the cold-soak rig reproduces the cold-weather axis in a freezer and in a real Canadian winter. The point is not to replicate NANOOK - it is to have bench-scale evidence on the same variables the trials measure.

Requirement Source 4: NRC + DRDC Counter-Drone Work

The NRC/DRDC counter-drone direction asks for a detection system that picks up the signature of a drone's rotating propellers without emitting any radio signal that could reveal its own location. That constraint - receive-only - is the interesting part, and it shapes two builds. The passive acoustic drone detector listens for rotor harmonics with a MEMS microphone array, rejects birds and aircraft, and estimates direction of arrival by beamforming. Its RF sibling, the passive radar node, detects aircraft by listening to reflections of someone else's broadcast transmitters - again emitting nothing. Both feed detections into the common operating picture, because a single passive sensor is a hint and two agreeing sensors are a track.

Requirement Source 5: Airspace Deconfliction

The Army is studying how to manage airspace shared by many uncrewed aircraft at once - the problem that appears the moment cheap drones stop being rare. The mini airspace deconfliction manager builds this at simulation scale: ESP32 beacons plus ArduPilot SITL simulated drones, dynamic geofences, conflict alerts, and a human-in-the-loop override. It also doubles as the traffic layer for the multi-domain common operating picture, since a fused air picture is exactly what a deconfliction cell would need to look at.

What Canada Is Building (and Where I Fit)

Sky: the Arctic Over-the-Horizon Radar (based on Australia's JORN design, adapted for RCAF/NORAD, initial capability due December 2029) will give long-range early-warning coverage of the northern approaches, feeding a layered picture of conventional radar, intelligence, airborne, and space-based sensors. Its companion program, Crossbow, is a classified Canada-US non-radar sensor network - you can't copy it and shouldn't try.

Water: the Canadian Arctic Sensor Suite Initiative aims for persistent surveillance from seabed to space. DRDC's Northern Watch demonstration ran into problems with installation, ice, technical issues, and data management. In DRDC-linked work at Dalhousie, uncrewed vehicles act as sensor hosts that dock at seabed nodes to recharge and download data.

The pattern in both: the hard parts are data management, fusion, and keeping sensors alive in harsh conditions - which is exactly where my DevOps and infrastructure background is an advantage. My builds target that layer: cheap nodes that survive the cold, buffer through dead links, sign their data, and fuse into one picture.

The Builds

Each build has its own page with the full step-by-step guide, parts list, and bench photos.

Flagship Sensor Nodes

  • Sky: Passive Aerial Early-Warning Node - KrakenSDR five-channel coherent receiver listening to FM/TV broadcast reflections off aircraft; emits nothing. RTL-SDR ADS-B receiver as ground truth, pyAPRiL for clutter filtering and DoA, scored against ADS-B.

  • Ground: Seismic & Acoustic Perimeter Mesh - solar LoRa mesh nodes with ADXL355 accelerometers (geophone upgrade path), edge classification of vehicles vs footsteps vs wind, with a rover or drone as a human-tasked verify asset.

Robots & Drones

  • GPS-Denied Navigation Rover - ROS 2 rover fusing IMU, wheel odometry, and LiDAR with an Extended Kalman Filter; GNSS gets cut and drift is measured against the u-blox ground truth.
  • Convoy-Following Robots on a Degraded Mesh - two rovers follow a lead over LoRa/Wi-Fi mesh, hold formation through link drops, and recover - the DDIL problem at bench scale.
  • Thermal Search-and-Rescue Drone - X500-class airframe with a FLIR Lepton thermal camera and Hailo AI HAT+ edge detection of people in snow and brush, pushed to a live map. Matches the Army's stated Arctic sovereignty and SAR-with-Rangers requirement.
  • Passive Acoustic Drone Detector - MEMS microphone array on a Pi detecting rotor harmonics, rejecting birds and aircraft, estimating direction of arrival by beamforming. Emits nothing; echoes the NRC/DRDC direction at hobby scale.
  • Mini Airspace Deconfliction Manager - ESP32 beacons plus ArduPilot SITL simulated drones, dynamic geofences, conflict alerts, and human-in-the-loop override.

Cold & Trust

  • Cold-Soak Characterization Rig - heated, instrumented battery bay tested in a freezer and real Canadian winter; publishes runtime, voltage sag, and failure data against temperature - a clean dataset on a stated Army pain point.
  • Secure Robot-Fleet Updates - secure boot, TPM 2.0, signed over-the-air updates, automatic rollback, tested in a cold chamber. Supply-chain integrity from the DDI, applied to my own fleet.

The Piece That Ties It Together

  • Multi-Domain Common Operating Picture - every node publishes signed detections over MQTT/Zenoh; a central Kalman-filter tracker fuses air, water, and ground tracks; nodes buffer through dead links and sync on reconnect; a human makes every decision. Chaos-tested by cutting links and killing nodes.

Workshop Setup and Safety

A program with this many parallel builds lives or dies on bench discipline, so the workshop got set up like a small lab rather than a desk with a soldering iron on it. The bench is split into three zones: a solder zone (iron, fume extraction, task light, heat-resistant mat), an RF zone (SDRs, antennas, and coax kept physically away from the soldering iron and the bench supply, because stray noise and ESD both kill RF front ends), and a clean assembly zone where drones and rovers get worked on without solder splatter or metal filings. The floor beside the bench is the robot test area - rovers never get powered with wheels on the bench.

ESD: the assembly zone has a grounded ESD mat and a wrist strap, and they get used every time a bare board is handled - the ADXL355 breakouts, the Hailo HAT+, and the KrakenSDR's RF inputs are all the kind of parts that die silently and fail mysteriously a week later. The strap gets a continuity check with the multimeter before the first session of the day, not just the first session ever.

LiPo charging station: the drone batteries have their own rules, written on an index card taped above the charger:

  • Charge only on a concrete or tile surface, inside a LiPo bag, with nothing flammable within a metre.
  • Never charge unattended - if I leave the room, the charger stops.
  • Never charge a cold or frozen pack; after a cold-soak or winter field run, the pack warms to room temperature first.
  • Balance charge, always; storage-charge to ~3.8 V per cell for anything sitting more than a few days.
  • Any pack that is puffed, dented, or crashed is retired to the discharge bucket, not back to the shelf.

Fume extraction: a small solder fume extractor with a carbon filter sits behind the iron, and rosin-core work also gets the window cracked - the filter slows the exposure, it does not eliminate it. Fire: an ABC dry-chemical extinguisher hangs within arm's reach of the bench (not across the room), and the LiPo bag lives beside the charger, not in a drawer. A LiPo fire is a class-B-ish metal-adjacent fire you do not fight with water; the plan is bag, distance, extinguisher if it is small, and the door if it is not.

Checkout procedure: every new board meets the multimeter before it meets power - continuity between the power rails to rule out a dead short, then a first power-up from the bench supply with the current limit set low, so a mistake costs a current-limited brown-out instead of a dead board. The bench supply's voltage display gets verified against the multimeter before I trust it for the day. That routine is written down as a checklist, because the day you skip it is the day you need it:

BENCH POWER-ON CHECKLIST

# Bench power-on checklist - run at the start of every work session
# 1. Bench supply OFF, voltage knob at minimum, current limit at minimum
# 2. Verify supply display against multimeter (set 5.00 V, compare readings)
# 3. ESD strap: continuity check strap -> mat -> ground point (< 1 Mohm expected)
# 4. New board: meter in continuity mode, check VCC-GND rails for shorts
#    - any beep on power rails = stop, find the short before powering
# 5. First power-up: set current limit to ~100 mA for a bare board,
#    ~500 mA for a Pi, never straight to full current
# 6. Power on, watch the current draw for 10 seconds before touching anything
#    - near-zero draw or pegged-at-limit both mean: power off, investigate
# 7. LiPo check: charger on concrete, LiPo bag open and ready,
#    extinguisher pin checked, nothing flammable within 1 m
# 8. Log the session start in the lab notebook (date, boards on bench, goal)

None of this is glamorous, and that is the point: the checklist exists so the boring safety steps happen on the exciting days too - the day the KrakenSDR arrives, the day the drone first arms.

Procurement Notes for Canada

The headline total of US$5,250-7,950 is only the start of the real number. Buying this program in Canada adds three layers on top: 13% HST in Ontario on essentially everything, shipping that is rarely free at this basket size for heavy items like chassis and batteries, and duties and brokerage on anything that crosses the border. The duty itself is often zero under the electronics tariff headings - the sting is the courier brokerage fee, a flat charge for "clearing" your own parcel that can add a meaningful percentage to a small US order. The practical rule: one consolidated cross-border order beats five small ones, and Canadian stock beats US stock even at a slightly higher sticker price.

What was bought where:

  • PiShop.ca - all four Pi 5 boards, the official 27 W power supplies, active coolers, the M.2 HAT, and Pi Camera Module 3. Canadian Pi stock with no border risk; this was the single largest domestic order.
  • RobotShop (Quebec) - the two 4WD rover chassis with encoders, the BTS7960 motor driver, and misc brackets. Domestic robotics stock, reasonable shipping to Ontario, and no brokerage surprises.
  • Digi-Key Canada - the ADXL355 breakouts' companion parts, INA228 modules, DS18B20 probes, connectors, and the resistor/cap kits. Digi-Key's Canadian site prices in CAD with duties handled at checkout, which makes the landed cost predictable.
  • Mouser Canada - the TPM 2.0 board and some RF connectors that nobody else had in stock domestically.
  • Amazon.ca - the unglamorous bulk: IP65/IP67 enclosures, cable glands, heat shrink, the ESD mat, the LiPo bags, the ABC extinguisher, and the heat gun. Not the cheapest per item, but fast and returnable when a box arrives the wrong size.
  • KrakenRF - the KrakenSDR and its five-antenna set sell through Crowd Supply and Mouser; this was the one unavoidable cross-border order, and it went through Mouser's Canadian checkout so the landed cost was known up front instead of being a roulette wheel at the door.
  • Direct - the two YubiKeys came from Yubico's store; security keys are not something to buy from a marketplace reseller.

Two Canada-specific traps worth recording. First, radio band plans: LoRa gear listed on marketplaces is often the 868 MHz European variant, and Canadian listings are not always labelled clearly - every board in Phase 3 was checked for 915 MHz before checkout. Second, winter shipping and batteries: LiPo and Li-ion packs have carrier restrictions, and a pack that sits in a frozen depot over a weekend arrives cold-soaked - which, per the safety rules, means it warms to room temperature before its first charge, not straight onto the charger.

Canada-Specific Notes

Radio rules: LoRa hardware is 915 MHz (North America) - 868 MHz parts are for Europe. Passive radar and AIS/ADS-B reception are receive-only, which is exactly what you want: never buy or build anything that transmits to interfere with signals. Jamming is illegal in Canada.
Drone rules: anything over 250 g must be registered and flown under a Transport Canada pilot certificate. The X500-class kit is well over that, so flights wait until the certificate, registration, and a NAV CANADA airspace check are done. Simulation (ArduPilot SITL + Gazebo) carries the early software work.
Batteries and cold: never charge a cold or frozen LiPo/Li-ion battery - let it warm up first. Every cold-soak run ends with the pack warming to room temperature before the charger connects.
Export control: the KrakenSDR team once pulled its passive-radar code because it appeared to fall under an export-control category covering passive radar. Before publishing radar code, check Canada's export control rules with Global Affairs Canada - or publish only the write-up, data, and metrics. Handling that carefully is itself a good signal to security reviewers.
Privacy and ethics: acoustic sensors are tuned to narrow-band machinery signatures (rotor harmonics, engine noise), not speech. Seismic and acoustic nodes run only on land I control and are never pointed at other people. Detection only - no jamming, no spoofing, no interception of restricted transmissions.

Build Log (Narrative)

The Did/Doing/Next list is the snapshot; this is the trail. Weeks are approximate - some ran long, some overlapped - but the order is real, and the order was the strategy.

Weeks 1-2 - the bench before the builds. Phase 0 arrived in a stack of boxes and the first two weeks went entirely into infrastructure: bench zones laid out, ESD mat grounded, network switch and OpenWrt router configured, the Pi 5s imaged onto NVMe instead of microSD, and the power-on checklist written down and actually followed. Nothing glamorous got built; everything later got easier. The lesson of the fortnight: imaging and inventorying ten builds' worth of compute up front is slower than doing it per-build, but only once.

Weeks 3-4 - something on the screen. The ADS-B kit was the first build to go live, deliberately: an RTL-SDR, a 1090 MHz antenna, and an evening of decoder setup put a real-time aircraft map on the dashboard. This mattered beyond morale - the ground-truth log started accumulating weeks before the radar processing chain existed, so by the time passive-radar detections appear there is already history to score them against.

Weeks 5-6 - simulation before hardware risk. While the cross-border orders were in transit, the software spine went up on the homelab cluster: ROS 2 on Ubuntu 24.04, Gazebo, and ArduPilot SITL. The drone's flight logic and the rover's EKF scaffolding were both written and exercised here, where a crash costs a restarted container instead of a broken airframe. This is also when the MQTT/Zenoh bus design was sketched, because every later node had to publish into it.

Weeks 7-8 - the radar problem gets real. The KrakenSDR arrived and bench bring-up started: five channels, phase calibration with the built-in noise source, and the slow realization that passive radar is mostly a data-engineering problem wearing an RF costume - moving IQ samples around fast enough, then doing the cross-correlation math. The pyAPRiL processing chain is the current long pole and is where the "Doing" list lives.

Weeks 9-10 - winter as free lab equipment. The cold-soak rig was assembled and the first freezer runs were logged: instrumented battery, temperature probes, power monitors, and a notebook of procedural mistakes that became the rig's checklist. A real cold snap doubled as the field trial. No dataset is published yet - that is a "Next" item, and it stays there until the numbers are worth publishing.

Weeks 11+ - everything in parallel. The program is now in its steady state: five workstreams advancing in parallel (radar processing, rover EKF tuning, hydrophone capture, LoRa mesh firmware, thermal pipeline), each documented on its own build page as it matures. The pattern that keeps working: hardware arrives, it gets benched against the checklist, it earns a simulation twin if it can hurt itself, and it does not get called "done" until there is a dataset or a dashboard to point at.

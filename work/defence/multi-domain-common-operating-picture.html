Multi-Domain Common Operating Picture

The fusion layer that ties every sensor build together: nodes publish signed detections over MQTT, buffer through dead links, a Kalman-filter tracker fuses air, water, and ground tracks, and a human makes every decision from one dashboard.

2026 Defence Software & Fusion In Progress

Overview

Every sensor build in my defence hardware program ends at the same question: now that a node sees something, where does that knowledge go? This project is the answer. It is the common operating picture (COP) that sits above the passive radar node, the hydrophone monitor, the seismic perimeter mesh, and the rest - one pipeline that takes signed detections from all of them, fuses them into tracks, and puts the result in front of a human.

The lesson from Canada's big sensor programs is that sensors are the easy part. The Arctic Over-the-Horizon Radar is designed to feed a layered picture of conventional radar, intelligence, airborne, and space-based sensors - the radar is one input, not the product. DRDC's Northern Watch demonstration ran into problems with installation, ice, technical issues, and data management. The hard part is data management, fusion, and staying alive on bad links. This project is that hard part, and it is where my DevOps background shows: brokers, buffers, signatures, chaos tests, and dashboards are what I do for a living.

The design rules are strict. Nodes sign every detection so a consumer can verify who said what. Nodes buffer locally and sync when links return, because a sensor that goes silent when the network drops is decoration. Fusion is time-first: clocks are disciplined from GNSS PPS before any tracker math is trusted. And the system cues, it never acts - every decision is made by a human looking at the dashboard.

The COP dashboard on my bench: the rugged laptop shows real-time ground, air, and water tracks with confidence scores, an alert queue, and live seismic and acoustic signal panels - fed by the solar LoRa sensor node sitting beside it.

The bus is deliberately thin. Nodes publish small signed JSON detections (node_id, timestamp, class, bearing/position estimate, confidence, signature) and nothing else; raw audio and RF captures stay on the node unless an operator pulls them. That keeps the LoRa and Wi-Fi links cheap and the broker load flat, and it means a dead link only delays data - it never loses it.

How I Built It

01
The bus: signed detections over MQTT

Every node publishes its detections to a Mosquitto broker over MQTT, with per-node Ed25519 keypairs generated at provisioning time. The payload is signed before it leaves the node, so any consumer - the tracker, the audit log, a future third party - can verify exactly which node said what with libsodium (PyNaCl on the Pi-side services, the Arduino Crypto library on ESP32).

I evaluated Zenoh as the alternative transport: its built-in store-and-forward and peer-to-peer mode are genuinely attractive for DDIL links, but MQTT's tooling, broker maturity, and the fact my existing services already speak it won the first iteration. Zenoh stays on the bench as the candidate for a brokerless version.

02
Time: GNSS PPS clock discipline

Multi-sensor fusion is meaningless without aligned timestamps - a 200 ms skew between a seismic node and an acoustic node can flip which detection the tracker thinks came first. Each aggregator node carries a u-blox M9N module, and its pulse-per-second output drives gpsd feeding chronyd with the PPS refclock, disciplining the system clock to well under a millisecond.

ESP32 mesh nodes without their own GNSS inherit time from their gateway over LoRa and stamp detections with both their local tick and the gateway-corrected estimate, so the tracker can weight them accordingly.

03
Store-and-forward: buffering through dead links

This is the DDIL pattern from my DDIL-Proof DevOps work applied to physical nodes. Each node keeps a local outbound queue - SQLite with the WAL journal on the Pi-class nodes, a ring buffer on high-endurance SD for the ESP32s - and publishes with MQTT QoS 1. When the link drops, detections pile up locally; when it returns, the node replays the backlog in order and the broker sees late data clearly marked by its original timestamps.

The whole behaviour was proven before any sensor went outside, with tc netem chaos tests on the bench: inject 40% packet loss, a 30-second full outage, or 2 s of latency, and confirm nothing is lost and nothing is acknowledged twice.

04
Fusion: one Kalman-filter tracker

A central Python service subscribes to every domain topic and runs a Kalman-filter tracker (built on FilterPy) that associates incoming detections with existing tracks. Air detections come in from the passive radar node and the acoustic drone detector, water contacts from the hydrophone monitor, and ground activity from the seismic mesh; each arrives as a position or bearing estimate with a covariance, so the filter can weigh a sharp bearing cut against a noisy position fix honestly.

The output is a single track table - track ID, state estimate, uncertainty ellipse, contributing sensors, and a confidence score - published back onto the bus so the dashboard and the audit log both consume the same truth.

05
Dashboard: one map, one alert queue, one human

The COP itself is a Leaflet map served from the homelab Kubernetes cluster, showing air, water, and ground tracks with their confidence scores and uncertainty, next to a live alert queue and per-node signal panels. Grafana covers the plumbing health behind it: broker lag, buffer depths, clock offsets, and node last-seen times.

The design constraint is deliberate: the system cues, it never acts. Alerts are presented with their confidence and evidence, and a human decides what - if anything - happens next. There is no automated response path, by design.

06
Audit: every alert keeps its evidence chain

Every alert carries its full evidence chain: the raw detection payloads it was fused from, the node signatures that authenticate them, the tracker version and parameters that produced it, and the operator's disposition. All of it is appended to the tamper-evident log from my Decision Black Box build - a hash-chained, append-only record where each entry includes the hash of the previous one, so silent edits are detectable.

07
Chaos testing: break it on purpose

The acceptance test for this system is failure. I cut links with tc netem, kill broker and tracker pods to let Kubernetes reschedule them, pull power on individual nodes, and corrupt one node's clock to watch the tracker down-weight its skewed detections instead of poisoning the picture.

The two metrics that matter are time-to-recover (how long until a domain is back on the map after its link or node dies) and track continuity (whether the same physical target keeps the same track ID across the outage). The protocol is scripted so the numbers can be re-run after every change, and the results will be published with the build.

Testing & Metrics

Testing runs at three levels, all scripted so they can be re-run after any change:

  • Link chaos: tc netem scenarios for loss, latency, jitter, and full outages of increasing duration. Target: zero lost detections and zero duplicate acknowledgements across any outage the node's buffer can hold.
  • Node and service failure: killed pods, pulled node power, and broker restarts. Target: the dashboard shows the domain as stale (not silent), and time-to-recover is measured from link restoration to first fused track update.
  • Clock corruption: one node's clock deliberately offset. Target: the tracker detects the skew (via timestamp residuals), down-weights that node, and the fused track stays continuous on the remaining sensors.
  • Track quality: track continuity (same physical target keeps the same ID across outages) and false-track rate during scripted scenarios with known ground truth.

Numeric results are in progress: the chaos harness and the metric collection are built, and the full published run set - time-to-recover, track continuity, and buffer behaviour per scenario - comes after the outdoor nodes are all feeding the bus. Nothing here is quoted until it is measured.

Defensive and consensual by design: every sensor feeding this picture is my own, deployed on my own property and aircraft. Nothing here touches other people's networks, signals, or land.
A human decides: the system detects and informs - it never acts. There is no automated response path anywhere in the design; every alert ends at a person.

GPS-Denied Navigation Rover

A ROS 2 rover that keeps navigating after GNSS is cut: wheel odometry, a BNO085 IMU, and 2D LiDAR fused with an Extended Kalman Filter, with position drift measured honestly against a u-blox ground-truth track.

2026 Defence Hardware In Progress

Overview

This build is a small tracked-class rover (a 4WD skid-steer metal chassis) that keeps an honest estimate of where it is after its satellite navigation is taken away. Wheel encoders, a BNO085 IMU, and a 2D LiDAR feed an Extended Kalman Filter running on a Raspberry Pi 5 under ROS 2. The u-blox M9N GNSS module rides along only as ground truth: I drive a known loop with GNSS on, then switch it off and measure how fast the estimated position drifts away from reality, expressed as error versus distance travelled.

Why this matters in Canada: DRDC is testing GPS-denied navigation, convoying, and cold-weather performance on exercises like Operation NANOOK, because GNSS jamming and denial are now baseline assumptions in any real operating area, and because the high Arctic adds weak satellite geometry on top. The Army's MINERVA challenges and the Defence Drone Initiative (launched July 23, 2026) both name ground robots that must keep working in exactly these conditions.

The build pattern is not mine. Published research (including work on arXiv) shows terrain-referenced and dead-reckoning localization for ground vehicles that fuses wheel odometry and IMU in GNSS-denied environments, with scan matching to arrest drift. This rover reproduces that pattern at hobby scale, on hardware already on my bench, with the drift numbers measured and published rather than assumed.

The key discipline: GNSS is not an input to the estimator. It is logged in parallel so that when I switch it off, the comparison between the EKF's belief and the last known true track gives a clean, honest drift measurement. The rover navigates the whole time on what it can sense on board.

How I Built It

01
Chassis and drive train

Assembled the metal 4WD chassis and wired the wheel encoders, the BTS7960 dual motor driver (one channel pair per side, skid-steer), the Li-ion pack with its BMS, and a DC-DC converter producing a clean 5 V/5 A rail for the Pi 5 and sensors. Motor power and logic power are deliberately separate so a motor stall can't brown out the compute.

A physical e-stop switch sits in series with motor power and nothing else: press it and the wheels are dead while the Pi keeps logging. Every test run starts with an e-stop check and a clear test area.

02
Software base

Ubuntu 24.04 Server on the Pi 5 8 GB, then ROS 2 Jazzy from the official apt repository. I wrote the robot_description URDF for the chassis (wheel positions, sensor frames, TF tree) and brought up the ros2_control diff-drive stack: diff_drive_controller commanding the motors through a custom hardware interface that talks PWM to the BTS7960, with the encoders feeding back joint states.

03
Sensors

The BNO085 breakout connects over UART (I2C as fallback) and publishes sensor_msgs/Imu with its on-chip fused orientation, which is far better than raw gyro integration at this price. The LD19 2D LiDAR runs through its ROS 2 driver and publishes LaserScan on /scan. The Pi Camera Module 3 records a visual log of each run so I can correlate drift events (a slip on gravel, a bump) with what actually happened.

04
Odometry fusion

The robot_localization package runs an Extended Kalman Filter (ekf_node) fusing wheel odometry (x, y, yaw velocities) with the BNO085's orientation and angular velocity, producing the odom to base_link transform. Tuning the process and measurement noise covariances is where the real work is: trust the IMU for heading, trust the encoders for short-range translation, and let the covariance grow honestly when inputs disagree.

05
LiDAR aiding

Wheel slip is the enemy of a skid-steer chassis, so the EKF alone will drift. To arrest it I add LiDAR aiding: rf2o_laser_odometry (dense scan-to-scan matching) publishes a LiDAR odometry estimate that becomes a third EKF input, and slam_toolbox runs alongside to maintain a map and a map-frame pose. The test protocol compares the same loop with LiDAR aiding on and off, so its contribution shows up directly in the drift numbers.

06
Ground truth and the cut

The u-blox M9N logs a GNSS track over a known loop (measured on the ground with markers) as ground truth. Once the fix quality is stable, I switch GNSS off on my own receiver and let the rover keep driving on odometry + IMU + LiDAR. The estimator never sees GNSS in either phase, so the logged comparison of EKF pose versus the true loop gives position drift as error versus distance travelled, with no ambiguity about where the truth came from.

07
Publish

Every run is recorded with ros2 bag record (all topics plus TF), and a small Python analysis script with rosbag2_py and matplotlib produces the drift plots: error versus distance, with and without LiDAR aiding. The dataset, the URDF, the EKF configuration, and the plots get published together so the numbers are reproducible, not just claimed.

Testing & Metrics

The test protocol, fixed before the runs so the numbers mean something:

  • Known loop: a measured closed loop on flat ground, marked at known distances, driven at a constant commanded speed.
  • Two configurations: EKF only (wheel odometry + IMU) and EKF + LiDAR aiding (rf2o / SLAM Toolbox), same loop, same speed.
  • Primary metric: position error versus distance travelled, sampled from the rosbag by comparing EKF pose against the u-blox M9N ground-truth track (and against the surveyed markers at the cut point).
  • Secondary metrics: heading error growth over time, loop-closure error at the return point, and IMU bias stability across a run.
  • Conditions log: surface, temperature, and battery state recorded per run, since all three move the numbers.

Results are in progress: the rig runs and logs, and the first drift datasets are being collected. No drift numbers are published yet because I won't quote figures I haven't measured. The dataset and plots go up with the build write-up once the loop runs are complete.

Safety & Legal Notes

Physical safety: the rover has a physical e-stop that cuts motor power, and every test run happens in a clear test area with the e-stop verified before the wheels spin.
GNSS legality: GNSS is only switched off on my own receiver. No jamming or spoofing of any kind - interfering with radio signals is illegal in Canada, and you don't need to interfere with anything to test denial: you just stop listening.
